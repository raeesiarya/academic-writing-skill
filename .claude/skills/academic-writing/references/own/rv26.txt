        Checking for Safety Specifications

      Abstract. In safety-critical domains such as autonomous driving, sys-
      tems must be evaluated across a large number of environment conditions,
      often represented as composite scenarios built from primitive scenar-
      ios. Existing statistical model checking (SMC) approaches analyze each
      composite scenario independently, requiring many expensive simulations
      and resulting in substantial redundant computation when scenarios share
      common structure. This work introduces a scenario-based compositional
      SMC framework for safety and co-safety specifications, enabling efficient
      analysis of composite scenarios. Our approach decomposes scenarios into
      primitives and specifications into sub-specifications, verifies each primi-
      tive independently, and composes the resulting statistical estimates using
      importance sampling and kernel density estimation. Our empirical eval-
      uation shows that the proposed framework can accurately answer verifi-
      cation queries for previously unseen composite scenarios while reducing
      simulation cost through parallelization and trace reuse.

      Keywords: Statistical Model Checking · Compositional Verification ·
      Scenario-Based Testing · Safety Specifications · Runtime Verification


1   Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) systems are increasingly deployed in safety-critical
domains, including autonomous driving, robotics, healthcare, and cyber-physical
systems in general. As these systems become more pervasive, ensuring their
safety and reliability remains a fundamental challenge. However, the complexity
of modern AI-based systems renders traditional verification techniques largely
inapplicable. Classical formal verification methods typically require an explicit
and tractable model of the system under analysis, an assumption that is often
violated in contemporary AI systems due to their inherent opacity.
    A practical alternative is to treat the system under analysis as a black box
and reason about its behavior through simulation-based analysis [20]. This ap-
proach is particularly prevalent in autonomous driving, where validating a sys-
tem through exhaustive real-world testing is infeasible. Instead, developers eval-
uate autonomous driving systems using a corpus of scenarios, either manually
2       A. Pomalapally and A. Raeesi et al.

designed by domain experts or extracted from real-world driving data, and assess
the system’s performance across these scenarios through repeated simulations.
    Statistical model checking (SMC) provides a principled framework for such
simulation-based verification [16]. SMC is a formal verification technique that
determines whether a stochastic system satisfies a given property within a par-
ticular scenario by analyzing a finite set of execution traces using statistical
methods such as hypothesis testing or confidence interval estimation. By miti-
gating the state-space explosion associated with exhaustive model checking, SMC
enables verification of systems whose complexity would otherwise make formal
analysis intractable. Nevertheless, SMC continues to face significant scalability
challenges. Obtaining meaningful and high-confidence verification results often
requires executing a large number of computationally expensive, long-running
simulations. Moreover, SMC must typically be repeated whenever the scenario
defining the environment conditions changes, leading to redundant computation.
    In this paper, we introduce a scenario-based compositional SMC framework
for safety and co-safety specifications, i.e., specifications whose violation or sat-
isfaction can be determined from finite executions. Our approach leverages both
scenario and specification decomposition. Given a composite scenario, we first
decompose it into a collection of primitive scenarios. Simultaneously, we decom-
pose the target safety specification into a set of sub-specifications. The black-box
system is then evaluated independently on each primitive scenario with respect
to all relevant sub-specifications. These verification tasks can be executed in par-
allel and require shorter simulation runs than the original monolithic verification
problem. Finally, we compose the resulting statistical estimates to obtain a verifi-
cation result for the original composite scenario and specification. Furthermore,
our compositional framework enables the reuse of traces generated for primi-
tive scenarios across multiple composite scenarios, thereby avoiding redundant
simulation effort and significantly reducing overall verification cost.
    We summarize our contributions as follows: (i) we introduce a novel compo-
sitional statistical model checking framework for safety and co-safety specifica-
tions; (ii) we implement the proposed framework and integrate it into VerifAI [9],
a toolkit for the formal design and analysis of AI-based systems; and (iii) we
conduct an empirical evaluation of the proposed approach and demonstrate its
effectiveness in improving verification efficiency while maintaining accuracy.


Related Work. To address the verification of AI-based systems, simulation-
based formal analysis has been widely adopted [20,9,1]. Specifically, statistical
model checking (SMC) [18,19,25,26,16,5,27,2] mitigates the state-space explosion
of exact formal methods by evaluating a finite set of execution traces, provid-
ing probabilistic guarantees bounded by statistical confidence intervals. Seshia
et al. [20] advocate for a compositional approach to scale up verification of AI
systems. Classic compositional verification (e.g., [6,7,4,14,22]) is not directly ap-
plicable to simulation-based verification of AI systems. Existing compositional
approaches for AI systems vary in their axes of decomposition; while some frame-
works decompose internal system architectures or neural network layers [21,10],
                Scenario-Based Compositional SMC for Safety Specifications          3

our work focuses on the spatial and temporal decomposition of environmental
scenarios and specifications.
    Dreossi et al. [8] has proposed a framework combining compositional anal-
ysis with simulation-based verification, but it focuses strictly on component-
level decomposition and targets active falsification rather than SMC. The closest
prior work to the proposed framework introduces a compositional approach to
SMC [24]. However, it is strictly restricted to Markovian, i.e., memoryless, spec-
ifications. Our new framework extends compositional SMC to non-Markovian
safety and co-safety specifications, overcoming a fundamental limitation.
    Another related line of work explores scenario decomposition, where en-
vironments and specifications are divided into primitive segments either spa-
tially [23,13] or temporally [3]. However, these differ significantly in their verifi-
cation methods: they rely on explicit white-box access [13], offline probabilistic
abstractions [23], or remain confined to falsification [3]. In contrast, our frame-
work achieves black-box compositional SMC for safety and co-safety properties.


2    Motivating Example




     (a) S                (b) X                  (c) O                   (d) C

Fig. 1: Primitive scenarios considered throughout the paper: a straight road seg-

    Consider evaluating an autonomous vehicle across various driving scenarios
(Figure 1). A common approach to constructing such scenarios is to use a set of
simpler primitive scenarios as building blocks for defining more complex com-
posite scenarios. Suppose that our primitive scenarios consist of a straight road
segment (S), an intersection (X), a roundabout (O), and a curved road segment
(C). Scenario description languages such as Scenic [11,12] support this style of
programmatic scenario construction through several composition operators. Se-
quential composition specifies the exact order in which scenarios are encountered
(e.g., S → X). Choice composition randomly selects one scenario from a set of
alternatives (e.g., choose(S, X, O, C)). Finally, shuffle composition randomly per-
mutes the order of a collection of scenarios (e.g., shuffle(S, X, O, C)).
    Our goal is to efficiently test the autonomous vehicle in composite scenar-
ios against safety and co-safety specifications, i.e., temporal specifications whose
satisfaction or violation can be determined from a finite execution. A monolithic
application of SMC treats each composite scenario independently. For every new
4       A. Pomalapally and A. Raeesi et al.

scenario and specification pair, SMC executes a large number of simulations and
analyzes the resulting traces. As the complexity of composite scenarios grows,
this process quickly becomes computationally expensive, requiring repeated ex-
ecution of long-running simulations that often share substantial structure.
    Our approach avoids this redundancy by shifting the computational effort to
the primitive scenarios. Instead of directly evaluating every composite scenario,
we execute the system independently within each primitive scenario and store
the resulting traces. We then evaluate these traces against all sub-specifications
induced by the target specification, corresponding to the sub-automata of its
monitor automaton. Given a composite scenario, we compose the previously
computed results to estimate the probability of satisfying the target specification.
    A key challenge is that the distribution of behaviors observed in an iso-
lated primitive scenario may differ from the distribution induced when that
same primitive appears as part of a larger composite scenario. To account for
this distributional mismatch, we employ importance sampling [15] together with
kernel density estimation (KDE). Intuitively, traces generated from primitive
scenarios are weighted according to their likelihood under the target composite
scenario. This enables us to estimate results for previously unseen composite
scenarios without actually running the system in those scenarios. Consequently,
our approach generates computationally efficient and highly accurate estimates
for SMC queries.


3   Method

In this section, we present our compositional approach for checking safety spec-
ifications. We assume that the controller under consideration is Markovian, i.e.,
it outputs actions that solely depend on the current state of the plant, and that
it operates in a Markov Decision Process (MDP). A primitive scenario defines
environment conditions from a set of initial states to a set of terminal states,
e.g., the primitive scenario in Figure 1b starts at the car’s initial position and
ends when the car leaves the intersection. A composite scenario is a composition
of such primitives, e.g., S → X sequentially composes S and X, defining a scenario
that starts at the beginning of a straight road and ends after an intersection.
    Let τ ∼ (π, S) denote a (labeled) trace sampled by running a policy π in a
scenario S, let T be a finite set of such traces, and let ϕ be a safety specification
in automaton form. SMC estimates the specification satisfaction probability as:

3.1   Sequential Composition

Consider the sequential composition S → X. Let T S and T X denote traces inde-
pendently generated for the primitive scenarios S and X, respectively. Our goal is
to use the primitive scenario traces T S and T X to estimate the satisfaction prob-
ability for the composite scenario S → X without actually generating traces for
it. Let τ S→X , τ S , and τ X denote traces sampled for S → X, S, and X, respectively.
Let µ0 be the initial automaton state distributions, and let µS→X , µS , and µX
denote the automaton state distribution induced by the scenarios S → X, S, and
X, respectively. Using Equation (2), we can compute the SMC query as follows:

throughout the given sequential composition, i.e., µ0 − → µS −→ µS→X .
    Equation (6) is a valid decomposition of the monolithic SMC query due to
the assumed Markovian dynamics of the underlying stochastic process, i.e., it is
sufficient to account for the distributional mismatch between the last and the
first MDP states of the scenarios S and X, respectively. In practice, it is crucial
to ensure that the output and input MDP state distributions of sequentially
composed scenarios like S and X overlap. We assume it to be the case, and thus,
we only account for the distributional mismatch through importance sampling.
As such, we estimate ιX and oS using Gaussian kernel density estimation (KDE),
an asymptotically valid nonparametric method. KDE performs well in moderate
dimensions, whereas it might suffer from the curse of dimensionality in high-
dimensional spaces. We expect to apply the proposed approach in moderately
sized semantic feature spaces; therefore, for our purposes, KDE suffices.

3.2   Choose Composition
Consider the choose composition choose(S, X). Let τ choose(S,X) , τ S , and τ X de-
note traces sampled for the composite scenario and the corresponding primitive
scenarios. The satisfaction probability can be written as a mixture distribution:
                                               1               1
               Pr[τ choose(S,X) |= ϕ] =          Pr[τ S |= ϕ] + Pr[τ X |= ϕ].                         (7)
                                               2               2
Intuitively, the composite scenario induces a probabilistic choice over the con-
stituent scenarios, and the overall satisfaction probability is obtained by marginal-
izing over the selected branch. This expression can be easily extended to n-ary
and weighted choose compositions by marginalizing with branch probabilities.

3.3   Shuffle Composition
Finally, consider the shuffle composition shuffle(S, X), which randomly picks
a permutation of the given scenarios to sequentially compose. Let τ shuffle(S,X)

                                         1                 1
             Pr[τ shuffle(S,X) |= ϕ] =     Pr[τ S→X |= ϕ] + Pr[τ X→S |= ϕ].      (8)
                                         2                 2
This can be trivially extended to n-ary and weighted shuffle compositions. While
shuffle composition results in a factorial growth in the number of permutations,
we expect the number of shuffled scenarios to remain relatively small.


4     Empirical Evaluation
In this section, we empirically evaluate the proposed framework along two di-
mensions: accuracy and efficiency. To evaluate accuracy, we impose a fixed trace
budget on both our compositional SMC approach and a monolithic SMC base-
line, and compare the resulting satisfaction-probability estimates against those
obtained by the baseline. To evaluate efficiency, we impose a fixed time bud-
get, limiting both approaches to generating traces within the allotted time. We
then compare the resulting estimates with their respective counterparts from the
fixed-trace-budget experiment, assessing whether each approach can converge to
accurate estimates under a limited data budget. This setting is particularly rel-
evant during early-stage system development, when rapid feedback is valuable.
    We implemented the proposed framework1 as a prototype integrated with
VerifAI [9], a toolkit for the formal design and analysis of AI-based systems. We
use MetaDrive [17] as the simulator and Scenic [11,12] as the scenario description
language. We test MetaDrive’s built-in expert policy, a neural controller trained
to handle general driving scenarios.
    We evaluate the framework on seven composite scenarios: (i) S → X, (ii) S
→ X → S, (iii) S → O → C, (iv) C → S → X → S, (v) C → X → S → X → C,
(vi) S → choose (C, X, O), and (vii) S → shuffle (C, X, O). These comprise five
sequential compositions of varying lengths and two compositions using choose
and shuffle. We consider four specifications: two safety properties, 2-Stop,
which forbids more than one near-stop event, and Tollgate, which forbids re-
maining below a slow-speed threshold for more than three consecutive steps;
and two co-safety properties, V-Shaped, which requires the ego to eventually
complete a fast–slow–fast speed profile, and Sustained, which requires the ego
to eventually sustain a large steering input for three consecutive steps. The four
specifications are detailed in Appendix A. This yields 28 scenario–specification
pairs, each evaluated in both experiments.

4.1    Evaluating Accuracy
In the fixed-trace-budget experiment, we generate 1000 composite scenario traces
for the monolithic SMC baseline. For our compositional SMC method, we instead
generate 1000 traces for each primitive scenario and reuse these traces across all
considered composite scenarios.

    Table 1 presents results for the Tollgate specification as an example. The
compositional estimates closely track the values reported by the monolithic base-
line, demonstrating the accuracy of our compositional framework. We observe
similar agreement for nearly all remaining scenario–specification pairs, as re-
ported in Table 2 of Appendix B. One notable exception is the Sustained spec-
ification for the S → shuffle (C, X, O) scenario, for which ∆ reaches +0.1162.
This gap arises from MDP initial-state correlation discussed in Section 3.1: in
the monolithic execution, each subsequent scenario begins from an MDP state
the previous scenario left off at, whereas the compositional model samples each
subsequent scenario from its own initial MDP state distribution. Although we
use importance sampling to account for this distributional mismatch, the quality
of the compositional estimate depends directly on the degree of overlap between
the MDP state distributions at the interfaces of consecutive scenarios. This high-
lights that the initial state distributions of primitive scenarios must be chosen
carefully to ensure accurate compositional estimates. One way we ensure this
in our experiments is by simulating distinct geometries of the same primitive
scenario to yield a representative state distribution. For example, we randomly
sample four-way intersections on the same map with varying initial velocities
and headings. By diversifying these initial conditions, we maximize the likeli-
hood that state distributions overlap across scenario boundaries. Finally, note
that the expert policy used in our experiments was not trained to satisfy any of
the considered specifications; rather, it serves as a placeholder.

4.2    Evaluating Efficiency
In the fixed-time-budget experiment, both the compositional approach and the
monolithic baseline are given a 15-minute wall-clock budget for trace generation.
Under this budget, the compositional estimates converge faster toward their re-
spective fixed-trace-budget references than the monolithic baseline, with a mean
deviation of 0.029 versus 0.039. This improvement is enabled by the composi-
tional method generating 14.6× more traces on average (ranging from 5.2× to
32.3× more traces). Figure 2 shows a representative scenario–specification pair.

    The gap is concentrated among pairs involving choose/shuffle scenarios,
where the per-trace cost is sufficiently high that the 15-minute budget yields as
few as 20 monolithic traces. The gap is largest for the ⟨V-Shaped, S → shuffle
(C, X, O)⟩ pair, for which the monolithic estimate has an error of 0.275 relative
to the reference, compared with 0.014 for the compositional approach. Even
when the time budget is extended to 60 minutes, the monolithic baseline still
achieves an error of 0.188, substantially higher than the error achieved by the
compositional approach with only one quarter of the time budget (15 minutes).
Full results for the 60-minute experiment are provided in Appendix C.2.
    Finally, we conduct a compute-matched ablation in which the monolithic
baseline retains the 15-minute budget, while the compositional approach re-
ceives an equal budget for each of the four primitive scenarios (S, X, O, C),
yielding 225 seconds per primitive. With the sample-count advantage removed,
the two approaches are essentially tied, with mean deviations of 0.038 and 0.039,
respectively, across the 28 scenario–specification pairs. This result further vali-
dates that the primary benefit of our approach comes from sample reuse: a single
pool of primitive traces can serve all composite scenarios, whereas the monolithic
baseline must regenerate traces independently for each composite scenario. Full
results are provided in Appendix C.3.


5   Conclusion

We presented a scenario-based compositional statistical model checking frame-
work for safety and co-safety specifications. By decomposing composite scenar-
ios into primitive scenarios and specifications into sub-specifications, our ap-
proach enables independent verification of primitive scenarios whose results are
10      A. Pomalapally and A. Raeesi et al.

then composed using importance sampling and kernel density estimation to an-
swer SMC queries about previously unseen composite scenarios. The framework
supports sequential, choose, and shuffle compositions. Our empirical evaluation
demonstrates that the proposed approach produces accurate estimates while re-
ducing simulation cost through parallelization and trace reuse—a particularly
valuable property in early-stage development when rapid feedback is essential.

C.3   Compute-Matched Ablation
A natural concern is whether the compositional method’s advantage is genuine
or merely a consequence of sample count: within a fixed wall-clock window it
generates many short primitive traces, whereas the monolithic baseline generates
only a few long ones. To control for this, we level the comparison by giving
both methods the same total simulation budget rather than the same wall-clock
time, charging the compositional method only the cost of generating a single
monolithic scenario and asking whether it still recovers the same estimates.
   Each primitive pool Pi (i = 1, . . . , k) contains Ni traces generated within
budget T , equal to the budget for one monolithic scenario. Splitting the same
budget evenly across the k primitives allocates T /k to each. Assuming a roughly
constant per-trace cost, this corresponds to the first ⌊Ni /k⌋ traces in each pool.
We therefore truncate every pool to this prefix, preserving a total budget of T
while leaving the estimator unchanged.
