# NLP corpus: style notes

Papers: Transformer (Vaswani et al. 2017), BERT (Devlin et al. 2019), LoRA (Hu et al. 2021), Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al. 2022), ROME (Meng et al. 2022), Extracting Training Data (Carlini et al. 2021).
These notes cover how the papers are written, not what they found. Every quoted fragment appears in `txt/` (allowing for whitespace and line-break differences).

---

## 1. Per-paper notes

### Transformer ("Attention Is All You Need")
- **Abstract** (~170 words). The moves come in strict order. First the status quo: "The dominant sequence transduction models are based on complex recurrent or convolutional neural networks". Then the method, in one sentence with a named artifact: "We propose a new simple network architecture, the Transformer, based solely on attention mechanisms". Then two headline numbers, each tied to a named benchmark and to the margin: "achieves 28.4 BLEU on the WMT 2014 English-to-German translation task, improving over the existing best results, including ensembles, by over 2 BLEU". The abstract ends on a generalization claim, not a sales pitch. There is no explicit gap sentence; the gap is implied by "dispensing with recurrence and convolutions entirely".
- **Intro** has only 4 paragraphs. Opening: "Recurrent neural networks, long short-term memory [13] and gated recurrent [7] neural networks in particular, have been firmly established as state of the art approaches". The gap is one crisp sentence that ends a paragraph: "The fundamental constraint of sequential computation, however, remains." The contribution is a single paragraph that starts "In this work we propose the Transformer". There are no bullets and no roadmap in the intro; the roadmap comes at the end of Background: "In the following sections, we will describe the Transformer, motivate self-attention and discuss its advantages".
- **Related work** ("Background") is short and thematic. It ends by positioning: "To the best of our knowledge, however, the Transformer is the first transduction model relying entirely on self-attention".
- **Method** defines notation inline at first use: "the encoder maps an input sequence of symbol representations (x1 , ..., xn ) to a sequence of continuous representations". Concrete hyperparameters sit in the prose ("a stack of N = 6 identical layers"). Design choices get an explicit "because": "We chose this function because we hypothesized it would allow the model to easily learn to attend by relative positions". The paper is honest about ties: "found that the two versions produced nearly identical results (see Table 3 row (E))". Hedges are attached to mechanisms: "We suspect that for large values of dk , the dot products grow large in magnitude". A footnote holds the supporting derivation for that suspicion.
- **Results** use a fixed comparison template: "outperforms the best previously reported models (including ensembles) by more than 2.0 BLEU". Cost appears as a second axis: "at a fraction of the training cost of any of the competitive models". The ablation prose walks the table row by row: "In Table 3 rows (B), we observe that reducing the attention key size dk hurts model quality." followed by "This suggests that determining compatibility is not easy". Table captions state the takeaway: "The Transformer achieves better BLEU scores than previous state-of-the-art models".
- **Conclusion** has 3 short paragraphs: restate, results, future work ("We are excited about the future of attention-based models"). There is no limitations section.
- **Do not copy**: the abstract reports EN-FR as 41.8 while the Results text says "achieves a BLEU score of 41.0". Numbers must agree across abstract, text and tables. The paper also uses "Transformer" and "transformer" inconsistently.

### BERT
- **Abstract** (~120 words). It names the method and expands the acronym at once: "We introduce a new language representation model called BERT, which stands for". It contrasts with prior work in the second sentence ("Unlike recent language representation models"), gives a two-adjective verdict ("BERT is conceptually simple and empirically powerful."), and then delivers four results. Each result has an absolute score and a delta in parentheses: "pushing the GLUE score to 80.5% (7.7% point absolute improvement)".
- **Intro** opens on broad established fact: "Language model pre-training has been shown to be effective for improving many natural language processing tasks". It then gives a taxonomy of two existing strategies ("feature-based and fine-tuning"), followed by an "argue" gap: "We argue that current techniques restrict the power of the pre-trained representations". The gap is sharpened with "The major limitation is that standard language models are unidirectional". The contributions are a 3-item bullet list introduced by "The contributions of our paper are as follows". Each bullet begins with a verb claim ("We demonstrate the importance of bidirectional pre-training"; "We show that pre-trained representations reduce the need for"). The last bullet is a bare result: "BERT advances the state of the art for eleven NLP tasks." There is no roadmap.
- **Related work** is grouped by theme under run-in headings (Unsupervised Feature-based / Fine-tuning / Transfer from Supervised Data). Positioning is done per paper inside the theme ("Similar to ELMo, their model is feature-based and not deeply bidirectional.") rather than in a closing sentence.
- **Method**: notation is declared in one sentence ("we denote the number of layers (i.e., Transformer blocks) as L, the hidden size as H"), followed by named configurations (BERTBASE, BERTLARGE). The paper defines its own terms: "Throughout this work, a “sentence” can be an arbitrary span of contiguous text". Each choice is motivated: "BERTBASE was chosen to have the same model size as OpenAI GPT for comparison purposes." Weaknesses of the paper's own design are acknowledged before the fix: "a downside is that we are creating a mismatch between pre-training and fine-tuning".
- **Claims**: "BERTBASE and BERTLARGE outperform all systems on all tasks by a substantial margin, obtaining 4.5% and 7.0% respective average accuracy improvement". In ablations, the paper strengthens a baseline in good faith: "In order to make a good faith attempt at strengthening the LTR system, we added a randomly initialized BiLSTM on top." A firm novelty claim is carefully bounded: "we believe that this is the first work to demonstrate convincingly that scaling to extreme model sizes also leads to large improvements on very small scale tasks". The surprise is flagged and hedged: "It is also perhaps surprising that".
- **Conclusion**: 3 sentences, one paragraph, no limitations.
- **Do not copy**: the conclusion says "benefit from deep unidirectional architectures" when the point is bidirectional, which is a typo in the paper's key message. The paper also mixes "% point absolute improvement" with "% absolute improvement" in a single sentence.

### LoRA
- **Abstract** (~200 words) uses the full move set. Paradigm: "An important paradigm of natural language processing consists of large-scale pretraining". Problem: "full fine-tuning, which retrains all model parameters, becomes less feasible". Concrete instance: "Using GPT-3 175B as an example". Method, named with its acronym: "We propose Low-Rank Adaptation, or LoRA, which freezes the pretrained model weights". Numbers as multiples: "reduce the number of trainable parameters by 10,000 times and the GPU memory requirement by 3 times". Quality parity: "performs on-par or better than finetuning in model quality". Secondary analysis, then artifact release.
- **Intro** opens on a broad dependency: "Many applications in natural language processing rely on adapting one large-scale, pre-trained language model". The gap comes in two layers: cost ("The major downside of fine-tuning is that the new model contains as many parameters as in the original model."), then the shortcomings of existing fixes ("these method often fail to match the fine-tuning baselines, posing a trade-off between efficiency and model quality"). The hypothesis is explicit and credited: "We take inspiration from Li et al. (2018a); Aghajanyan et al. (2020)" … "We hypothesize that the change in weights during model adaptation also has a low “intrinsic rank”". The contributions are framed as "LoRA possesses several key advantages." followed by 4 bullets, each pairing a benefit with its mechanism. A separate "Terminologies and Conventions" paragraph fixes notation up front ("We use r to denote the rank of a LoRA module.").
- **Unusual structure**: a whole section titled as a question, "AREN’T EXISTING SOLUTIONS GOOD ENOUGH?", builds the gap with a measurement (Table 1 latency) before the method appears. Sub-headings are claims ("Adapter Layers Introduce Inference Latency", "Directly Optimizing the Prompt is Hard"). The analysis section opens with numbered research questions ("We perform a sequence of empirical studies to answer the following questions").
- **Method** motivates choices in two ways. Some are justified by convenience, and the paper admits it: "both for simplicity and parameter-efficiency". Some are justified by an argument: "As a result, we simply set α to the first r we try and do not tune it." Limitations sit inside the method section: "LoRA also has its limitations. For example, it is not straightforward to batch inputs".
- **Claims and evidence**: before/after numbers are given with units ("we reduce the VRAM consumption during training from 1.2TB to 350GB"), and fairness of comparison is stated: "To ensure a fair comparison, we make two crucial changes to how we evaluate LoRA when comparing with adapters." Surprise is flagged ("Table 6 shows that, surprisingly, LoRA already performs competitively with a very small r"), followed by a hedged interpretation ("This suggests the update matrix ∆W could have a very small “intrinsic rank”") and then a bound on generality ("However, we do not expect a small r to work for every task or dataset."). Out-of-scope items are labeled as such: "While a thorough investigation into this phenomenon is out-of-scope for this work, we suspect".
- **Related work** is thematic, with bold run-in headings. Each theme ends by positioning: "The key functional difference is that our learned weights can be merged with the main weights during inference"; "to the best of our knowledge, none of these works considers low-rank update to a frozen model". The section closes with "In sum, we believe that our proposed low-rank adaptation update is well-motivated by the literature."
- **Conclusion**: one paragraph that restates the problem and the method, then numbered future-work questions.
- **Do not copy**: the typos ("Infernece", "instrisic", "comtenporary", "these method"), first-person tics like "we hope to answer if", and contributions phrased as product features. That style suits a systems paper but undersells the analysis.

### Chinchilla ("Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models")
- **Abstract** (~180 words). It opens with the question as the paper's goal: "We investigate the optimal model size and number of tokens for training a transformer language model under a given compute budget." Then the headline finding, stated flatly: "We find that current large language models are significantly undertrained". The scale of evidence comes in numbers ("By training over 400 language models ranging from 70 million to over 16 billion parameters"). The rule is given in quotable form: "for every doubling of model size the number of training tokens should also be doubled". The validation model follows, with a comparison list that gives each rival's size in parentheses. It closes on one highlight number.
- **Intro** opens on the trend: "Recently a series of Large Language Models (LLMs) have been introduced". The gap is framed as disagreement with a single named predecessor, with that paper's numbers quoted against the new ones: "they suggests that the size of the model should increase 5.5× while the number of training tokens should only increase 1.8×. Instead, we find that model size and the number of training tokens should be scaled in equal proportions." The research question is in italics-style quoted form: "In this work, we revisit the question: Given a fixed FLOPs budget,". There is no bullet list of contributions. Figure 1 is the result preview, and its caption states the conclusion.
- **Related work** is thematic. The differences from Kaplan et al. are enumerated: "Our work differs from Kaplan et al. (2020) in several important ways." followed by "First," and "Secondly,".
- **Method**: the three approaches are numbered and named in section titles ("Approach 2: IsoFLOP profiles"). The paper triangulates and reports agreement as evidence: "We find that the three approaches, despite using different fitting methodologies and different trained models, yield comparable predictions". Choices are justified in short clauses ("The Huber loss (𝛿 = 10−3 ) is robust to outliers, which we find important for good predictive performance").
- **Claims**: comparisons give absolute values on both sides ("Chinchilla achieves a perplexity of 7.16 compared to 7.75 for Gopher"), counts of wins and losses ("performing better on 51/57 individual tasks, the same on 2/57, and worse on only 4/57 tasks"), and failures named explicitly ("On four tasks (college_mathematics, econometrics, moral_scenarios, and formal_logic) Chinchilla underperforms Gopher"). Confounds are flagged against its own headline result: "Some caution is needed when comparing Chinchilla with Gopher on these language modelling benchmarks". Figure and table captions use a `Title | takeaway` form: "Figure 1 | Overlaid predictions."
- **Hedging** is calibrated by distance from the data: "While there is significant uncertainty extrapolating out many orders of magnitude, our analysis clearly suggests". Speculation is labeled as such: "Speculatively, we expect that scaling to larger and larger datasets is only beneficial when the data is high-quality."
- **Limitations** sit in "Discussion & Conclusion" as a concrete list (only two large runs, the power-law assumption, under one epoch). The paragraph closes by turning back to what the results do support: "Despite these limitations, the comparison of Chinchilla to Gopher validates our performance predictions".
- **Do not copy**: the abstract says 67.5% MMLU while the body says "an average accuracy of 67.6%". There is a doubled word ("4× more more data"), GPT-3 is given as "170 billion" in the discussion against 175B elsewhere, and "%" is used for percentage-point gains without saying so.

### ROME ("Locating and Editing Factual Associations in GPT")
- **Abstract** (~180 words) is built as hypothesis, then test. It leads with the finding: "We analyze the storage and recall of factual associations in autoregressive transformer language models, finding evidence that these associations correspond to localized, directly-editable computations." The two methods appear in sequence ("We first develop a causal intervention"; "we modify feedforward weights to update specific factual associations"). Results are qualitative here, with no numbers. The close is calibrated: "Our results confirm an important role for mid-layer feed-forward modules" and "suggest that direct manipulation of computational mechanisms may be a feasible approach".
- **Intro** opens with a question: "Where does a large language model store its facts?" The second sentence answers it: "In this paper, we report evidence that factual associations in GPT correspond to a localized computation that can be directly edited." A concrete running example follows immediately ("The Space Needle is located in the city of"). The gap: "the way that they store knowledge remains under-explored." The two-part approach is announced as "We use two approaches." and then enumerated with "First," and "Second,". Section numbers are woven into the contribution sentences ("(Section 3.2)", "(Section 3.3)") instead of a separate roadmap.
- **Method** introduces formal notation compactly ("We represent each fact as a knowledge tuple t = (s, r, o)"). Metrics are defined with their abbreviations at first use ("Our Efficacy Score (ES) is the portion of cases for which"). The hypothesis gets a named subsection ("The Localized Factual Association Hypothesis"). Epistemic verbs are graded on purpose: "we posit", "We conjecture", "We hypothesize", "we propose that this picture is complete". Negative results are reported: "we found this effect to be noisy and uninformative, in line with results by Vig et al. (2020b)."
- **Claims**: there are explicit failure-mode labels ("(F1) overfitting to the counterfactual statement" and "(F2) underfitting"), each baseline is assigned to one, and human-evaluation results are reported both ways, including where ROME loses: "rating ROME as 1.3 times less likely to be more fluent than the FT+L model". Novelty is marked contrastively: "is unsurprising, but their emergence at an early site at the last token of the subject is a new discovery."
- **Limitations** get their own subsection (3.7), stated as scope: "it only edits a single fact at a time, and it is not intended as a practical method". Ethics gets a separate section.
- **Related work** is thematic. Each theme ends by contrasting purpose: "Different from prior work, we do not strive to extract the most knowledge from a model, but rather wish to understand mechanisms".
- **Conclusion**: one paragraph, "We have clarified information flow during knowledge recall in autoregressive transformers, and we have exploited this understanding to develop a simple, principled model editor called ROME."

### Extracting Training Data (Carlini et al., security venue)
- **Abstract** (~150 words). Setting: "It has become common to publish large (billion parameter) language models that have been trained on private datasets." Then the demonstration claim and the concrete artifacts extracted (names, phone numbers, UUIDs). The surprise is stated as a condition: "Our attack is possible even though each of the above sequences are included in just one document". One evaluative adverb carries the risk: "Worryingly, we find that larger models are more vulnerable than smaller models."
- **Intro** builds the gap as a refuted belief: "The association between overfitting and memorization has— erroneously—led many to assume", and it quotes the "prevailing wisdom". The run-in "Contributions." paragraph starts with the flat claim "we demonstrate that large language models memorize and leak individual training examples", names the "key insight" in one sentence, and previews exact numbers ("find that over 600 of them are verbatim samples"; "In the best attack configuration, 67% of candidate samples are verbatim training examples").
- **Definitions** are formal and numbered ("Definition 2 (k-Eidetic Memorization)"). Each is followed by intuition and edge cases ("This definition admits pathological corner cases.", in a footnote). The paper motivates the choice of target: "We choose to attack GPT-2 to minimize real-world harm".
- **Method** is narrated as baseline, then weaknesses, then improvements. It starts with "We begin with a simple strawman baseline", diagnoses "This initial approach has two key weaknesses that we can identify.", and the next section answers each weakness.
- **Claims** pair counts with rates ("we identify 604 unique memorized training examples from among the 1,800 possible candidates, for an aggregate true positive rate of 33.5%"). Bounds are stated honestly: "Note that this is likely an extremely loose lower bound."
- **Lessons/Discussion**: claim-titled run-in paragraphs ("Larger Models Memorize More Data.", "Memorization Does Not Require Overfitting.", "Extraction Attacks Are a Practical Threat."). Each heading is a takeaway in sentence form. Mitigations are evaluated critically, not just listed: "Overall, sanitizing data is imperfect—some private data will always slip through".
- **Do not copy**: rhetorical adverbs aimed at the reader ("Worryingly", repeated "Interestingly", "vastly underestimates") would be out of place in a systems or accelerator paper unless the paper's point is risk. The background section is fragmented across the threat model, which makes it hard to follow.

## 2. Cross-cutting observations by dimension

**Abstract length and shape.** Abstracts run 120–200 words in one paragraph. Five of six include 2–4 specific numbers, each attached to a named benchmark or baseline. ROME is the exception: it is an analysis paper with qualitative claims. Numbers take three forms: absolute score plus margin (Transformer, BERT), multiplicative factors ("10,000 times", "4× more"), or counts ("hundreds of verbatim text sequences"). The method gets a proper name in the method sentence of every abstract.

**Intro openings.** Four open with a broad, cited statement of the field's current state (Transformer, BERT, LoRA, Chinchilla). ROME opens with a question. Extracting opens with a trend that has a risk attached. None opens with a motivational cliché or a dictionary definition. Every intro is short: 4–7 paragraphs, and every one fits within about 1.5 pages.

**Gap statements.** The gap is a single, specific, often contrarian sentence: "The fundamental constraint of sequential computation, however, remains."; "We argue that current techniques restrict the power"; "We find that current large language models are significantly undertrained"; "has— erroneously—led many to assume". Strong papers name the belief they are overturning.

**Contributions.** The format varies. BERT and LoRA use bullets; Extracting uses a run-in "Contributions." paragraph; Transformer, Chinchilla and ROME use prose. Every contribution is a verb claim ("We demonstrate", "We show", "we propose") and not a topic (for example, `We study X`).

**Roadmaps.** Roadmaps are rare. Only Transformer has one, and it sits at the end of Background. ROME embeds section pointers in the contribution prose instead. A standalone `The rest of this paper is organized as follows` paragraph is absent from all six papers.

**Comparisons.** The dominant template is `[Ours] outperforms [named baseline] by [Δ unit] on [benchmark]`, or `[value] compared to [value] for [baseline]`. Baselines are always named models or systems ("Gopher", "ConvS2S", "FT+L"), never `existing methods`. Paper-coined acronyms for baselines (FT, FT+L, KE, AdapterH) are defined once and then used consistently.

**Figures/tables in prose.** References are parenthetical or sentence-initial and point to the exact row or panel: "(see Table 3 row (E))", "Figure 3 (left)", "In Table 3 rows (A)". Captions carry the conclusion, not just a description.

**Hedging.** The papers sort claims into graded tiers:
- Observed facts are stated flatly, using "we find", "we observe", "we show".
- Interpretations of those observations are hedged with "This suggests", "indicating", "potentially".
- Mechanism guesses are marked with "We suspect", "we hypothesize", "we conjecture".
- Extrapolations are marked with "Speculatively", "significant uncertainty".

Firm claims are bounded with scope: "to the best of our knowledge", "on these datasets".

**Sentence level.** Sentences are typically 15–30 words. Voice is first-person plural active ("We train", "We find"), and setup details often drop into passive ("Sentences were encoded using byte-pair encoding"). Paragraphs open with a topic sentence that states the point or the question ("We turn our attention to the effect of rank r on model performance."). Run-in bold or italic headings ("Label Smoothing", "Practical Benefits and Limitations.") are used heavily in method, related-work and discussion sections. Footnotes carry derivations, hardware constants and caveats ("We used values of 2.8, 3.7, 6.0 and 9.5 TFLOPS for K80, K40, M40 and P100"), which keeps the main text clean.

**Conclusions.** Conclusions are short: 1–3 paragraphs, sometimes three sentences (BERT). Limitations live in a dedicated subsection (ROME, LoRA) or in the discussion (Chinchilla), not in the final summary. The final paragraph points forward: future work, or a broader implication.

## 3. Cross-paper patterns: transferable rules

1. **Name the method once, early, and expand it at once.** "We propose Low-Rank Adaptation, or LoRA, which freezes the pretrained model weights" (LoRA); "a new language representation model called BERT, which stands for" (BERT). After that point, use the name and nothing else.

2. **Every abstract number has a referent and a margin.** "achieves 28.4 BLEU on the WMT 2014 English-to-German translation task, improving over the existing best results, including ensembles, by over 2 BLEU" (Transformer); "pushing the GLUE score to 80.5% (7.7% point absolute improvement)" (BERT).

3. **State the gap as one specific sentence, ideally the belief being overturned.** "The fundamental constraint of sequential computation, however, remains." (Transformer); "We find that current large language models are significantly undertrained" (Chinchilla); "has— erroneously—led many to assume" (Extracting).

4. **Phrase contributions as verb claims, not topics.** "We demonstrate the importance of bidirectional pre-training for language representations." (BERT); "we demonstrate that large language models memorize and leak individual training examples" (Extracting).

5. **Quote the competitor's numbers against yours when you disagree.** "they suggests that the size of the model should increase 5.5× while the number of training tokens should only increase 1.8×. Instead, we find" (Chinchilla).

6. **Name baselines and comparison targets concretely.** "Chinchilla achieves a perplexity of 7.16 compared to 7.75 for Gopher" (Chinchilla); "BERTLARGE outperforms the authors’ baseline ESIM+ELMo system by +27.1%" (BERT).

7. **Give each design choice its reason in the same sentence.** "We chose the sinusoidal version because it may allow the model to extrapolate" (Transformer); "BERTBASE was chosen to have the same model size as OpenAI GPT for comparison purposes." (BERT).

8. **Grade your verbs by how close the claim is to the data.** Use flat verbs for observations ("We observe a significant performance drop when we use more than 256 special tokens", LoRA). Hedge interpretations ("This suggests that determining compatibility is not easy", Transformer). Label mechanism guesses ("We suspect that for large values of dk", Transformer) and extrapolations ("Speculatively, we expect", Chinchilla).

9. **Report where you lose, by name.** "On four tasks (college_mathematics, econometrics, moral_scenarios, and formal_logic) Chinchilla underperforms Gopher" (Chinchilla); "suggesting that ROME introduces some loss in fluency" (ROME).

10. **Show the comparison was fair, and strengthen weak baselines.** "To ensure a fair comparison, we make two crucial changes" (LoRA); "In order to make a good faith attempt at strengthening the LTR system" (BERT).

11. **Make captions and headings carry the takeaway.** In captions: "The Transformer achieves better BLEU scores than previous state-of-the-art models" (Transformer, Table 2). In headings: "Adapter Layers Introduce Inference Latency" (LoRA); "Memorization Does Not Require Overfitting." (Extracting).

12. **Point to the exact table row or figure panel.** "(see Table 3 row (E))" (Transformer); "Figure 3 (left)" (Chinchilla); "Figure 1fg and Figure 2bc" (ROME).

13. **End each related-work theme by saying how you differ.** "The key functional difference is that our learned weights can be merged with the main weights during inference" (LoRA); "Different from prior work, we do not strive to extract the most knowledge from a model" (ROME).

14. **State limitations as concrete scope, then say what still holds.** "it only edits a single fact at a time, and it is not intended as a practical method" (ROME); "Despite these limitations, the comparison of Chinchilla to Gopher validates our performance predictions" (Chinchilla).

15. **Keep conclusions short and forward-looking; leave the roadmap out of the intro.** BERT concludes in three sentences. Transformer puts its one roadmap line at the end of Background: "In the following sections, we will describe the Transformer". No paper in this set uses a full `rest of the paper is organized` paragraph.

**Anti-patterns these papers show that the skill should catch.** Numbers that disagree between the abstract and the body (Transformer: 41.8 vs 41.0; Chinchilla: 67.5% vs 67.6%). Unlabeled percentage-point versus relative gains. Typos in key-message sentences (BERT's "deep unidirectional architectures"). Reader-directed emotive adverbs ("Worryingly", "Remarkably") used without restraint. Inconsistent capitalization of the core term.
